Every well produces at the set constant rate. The gap between the isolated-well line and the field lines is the interference from neighbouring wells and, with closed boundaries, depletion. Where the field lines cross the minimum flowing pressure, the set rate can no longer be held.

Drawdown around a single well (infinite-acting)

Line-source solution for one well on its own. The pressure disturbance spreads outward with time; the radius of investigation marks roughly where it becomes noticeable.

NPV against number of wells

In a homogeneous, fully connected reservoir every well pattern eventually recovers the same oil. More wells bring it forward in time. The optimum is where the value of producing sooner no longer pays for another well.

Gas recovery comes from the p/z material balance: each well depletes its share of the gas until the average pressure can no longer push gas into the well at the minimum flowing pressure, or the well reaches its economic limit. More wells mainly bring production forward; they also recover a little more within the field life.

What the tool does

The tool models how producing wells pull down the pressure around them in a uniform reservoir, how the pressure disturbances from neighbouring wells overlap, and what that means for how many wells a field needs.

Pressure field: line source with superposition

Each well is treated as a line source in an infinite, homogeneous, isotropic reservoir of uniform thickness, producing a slightly compressible single-phase fluid at constant rate (field units):

Δp(r,t) = 70.6 · q·B·μ / (k·h) · E1( 948 · φ·μ·ct·r² / (k·t) ) [psi; q STB/d, t hours, r ft]

Pressures from several wells add together (superposition). At the wellbore, the skin pressure drop 141.2·q·B·μ·s/(k·h) is added.

With closed boundaries, the field is a no-flow rectangle. The tool uses the rectangle's Green's function, which is the product of two one-dimensional solutions (Newman's product method). It uses image wells at early time and a cosine series at late time, and integrates numerically over time. At late time this reproduces pseudo-steady-state depletion: average pressure falls by q·B·t / (Vp·ct).

Gas: real-gas pseudo-pressure

For gas the same solution is written in terms of the real-gas pseudo-pressure, m(p) = ∫ 2p/(μz) dp, which makes the flow equation close to linear:

m(pi) − m(p) = 711 · q·T / (k·h) · E1( 948 · φ·μgi·cti·r² / (k·t) ) [psi²/cP; q Mscf/d, T °R] Wellbore: add 1422·q·T·(s + D·q)/(k·h) (D = non-Darcy coefficient, d/Mscf)

Gas properties come from gas gravity and temperature:

  • pseudo-critical properties from the Sutton correlation;
  • z from Hall–Yarborough;
  • viscosity from Lee–Gonzalez–Eakin;
  • cg from the slope of z.

Total compressibility = Sg·cg + Sw·cw (3×10⁻⁶) + rock compressibility. Diffusivity uses gas properties at initial pressure, which is the usual approximation; it becomes less accurate late in depletion, when gas viscosity and compressibility have changed a lot.

Economics for gas: GIIP = A·h·φ·(1−Sw)/Bgi, with Bgi = 0.02827·z·T/p (ft³/scf). Each well's average pressure follows the p/z material-balance line, p/z = (pi/zi)·(1 − Gp/G), for its share G of the gas in place. Rate comes from the PSS deliverability in pseudo-pressure, including non-Darcy flow, capped at the maximum well rate. Technical recovery is the gas produced by the time average pressure falls to the minimum flowing pressure.

The model assumes dry gas, with no condensate, water production, liquid loading or compression stages.

Spacing and economics

For each well count n, the field area is split into n equal drainage areas. The productivity index uses the Dietz pseudo-steady-state form, with shape factor C_A = 30.88 for a well at the centre of a square:

J = k·h / ( 141.2·B·μ·[ ½·ln( 4A / (1.781·C_A·rw²) ) + s ] ) [STB/d/psi]

Recoverable oil is STOIIP × recovery factor, shared equally between wells. STOIIP = 7758·A·h·φ·(1−Sw)/Bo, with A in acres. Each well produces at the lower of J·(pi − pmin) and the maximum well rate. Rate then declines exponentially as average pressure falls, which assumes average pressure falls in proportion to cumulative production (a "tank" model). Wells come on stream at the set drilling rate. A well stops when its net revenue no longer covers its fixed opex, or at the end of field life. NPV is discounted monthly at mid-period and includes facilities, well costs, royalty and taxes, and opex.

Limitations

  • Single phase: oil above the bubble point with constant properties, or dry gas. There is no gas cap, aquifer, water injection or relative permeability.
  • Homogeneous and isotropic, with a full-thickness vertical completion and no layering, faults or kv/kh effects.
  • For oil, the recovery factor is an input and does not change with spacing. In real reservoirs, denser spacing often raises recovery through better contact with heterogeneous or compartmentalised rock. The tool shows acceleration value only, so it tends to favour fewer wells.
  • Wells are on a regular pattern with equal rates and equal drainage areas.
  • Use it for screening and first-pass spacing. Check development decisions with reservoir simulation.
